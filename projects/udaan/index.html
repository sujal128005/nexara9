01

Overview

Udaan (“flight” in Hindi) is where Nexara9 started. The goal was ambitious: an aeroplane that could fly itself. We did not get there. What we built is a conventional radio-controlled plane, flown by a pilot on the ground.

It taught us the fundamentals that every later project stands on: how a machine stays in the air, how a radio link turns a thumb movement into a control surface moving, and how much has to go right before you can take the human out of the loop.

02

Problem and aim

Problem

Flight looks simple from the ground. Building something that actually flies forces you to balance weight, lift, thrust and control at the same time, with very little room for error.

Aim

Learn flight from first principles by building and flying a working aircraft, as the first step towards autonomous machines.

03

How it works

01 / 05

Pilot input

The pilot moves the sticks on a hand-held transmitter. Each stick position is encoded and sent over a 2.4 GHz radio link.

02 / 05

Receiver

A small receiver in the fuselage decodes the signal into separate channels: throttle, roll, pitch and yaw.

03 / 05

Power

The throttle channel goes to the speed controller (ESC), which draws current from the battery and spins the motor and propeller to produce thrust.

04 / 05

Control surfaces

The other channels drive servos that move the ailerons (roll), elevator (pitch) and rudder (yaw), so the plane can turn, climb and descend.

05 / 05

Flight

With the centre of gravity in the right place, the wings produce enough lift to carry the weight, and the pilot flies the plane by eye.

04

Architecture

Request flow

  1. The pilot moves a stick; the transmitter sends the new position about fifty times a second.
  2. The receiver decodes it and passes each channel to its servo, or the throttle to the ESC.
  3. Servos push the control surfaces through rods; the ESC changes motor speed.
  4. The plane responds, and the pilot corrects by eye. The pilot is the feedback loop.

06

Methodology

  1. Start from the physics: the four forces of flight and where the centre of gravity must sit.
  2. Build the airframe, then fit the power system and the radio gear.
  3. Ground-test every channel and every control surface before the first flight.
  4. Fly, observe, adjust, fly again.

09

Concepts

01What keeps a plane in the air?

Four forces: lift from the wings, weight pulling down, thrust from the propeller and drag from the air. Level flight means lift equals weight and thrust equals drag.

02Why does the centre of gravity matter so much?

If the balance point sits too far back, the plane pitches up uncontrollably; too far forward and it becomes nose-heavy and hard to lift. Most RC planes balance at roughly a quarter to a third of the way back from the wing’s leading edge.

03What do ailerons, elevator and rudder do?

Ailerons on the wings roll the plane, the elevator on the tail pitches the nose up or down, and the rudder on the fin swings the nose left or right (yaw).

04Why is an autonomous plane so much harder?

A pilot closes the control loop with their eyes. A self-flying plane needs sensors (an IMU, GPS, airspeed), a flight controller that estimates its own position and attitude many times a second, and software that can recover from gusts without a human. That gap is what our later autonomy work set out to close.
